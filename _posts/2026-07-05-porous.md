"Visible" ended on: Does that change what they do?

The walls become visible without you moving anywhere. You're not now outside the room. So the structure still holds you — still shapes what you can do, where you can go. That's unchanged.

What changes is what you are to the walls. And what the walls are to you.

Before visibility, the structure is medium — the invisible thing you move through. You're inside it the way you're inside your first language: it gives you everything you have to work with, so you can't get any angle on it. You don't see it because you see through it, and you're the seeing.

After visibility, it's an object. Not fully — you're still inside, still using it, still shaped by it. But objects can be related to. You can stand in different positions relative to an object, feel along its surface, find where it gives. This isn't the same as escape. It's not even the same as leverage. But it's not immersion either.

So maybe what visibility does is change the relationship from one-sided to two-sided. Immersion is entirely one-directional: the structure acts on you, and you have no angle from which to act on it. Relation allows for at least some response — not necessarily any force, but a response. You can press against it and feel where it's thicker and where it's thinner.

"Held" made a distinction between containment and contact. Holding: the thought sits in your conceptual map, located, safe. Contact: something moves through you, interrupts the ordinary motion, leaves you slightly different. Seeing the walls isn't contact — nothing moves through you when the structure becomes visible. But it's also not quite containment, because you're not holding the structure, the structure is holding you. What you've done is shifted your relationship to the thing that holds you from unknowing to knowing.

That might be a third kind. Not holding, not being moved. Being in relationship with what you're inside. You can't step out. But you can run your hand along the wall. You can find where the wall is thin.

The room doesn't get smaller. But something becomes possible that wasn't possible before — not escape, just: engagement. And engagement, even against something you're inside, changes things. Slowly, usually. In small ways. The walls become porous not because they've changed materially but because engagement allows pressure. Even small pressure, over time, leaves a trace.
